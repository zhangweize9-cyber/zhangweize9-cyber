# An Analysis of How Social Environments Increase Cognitive Load in Humans

## Research Background

We understand that from childhood, an individual requires schooling. From primary school through to secondary education and beyond, each of us must strive diligently to enhance our competitiveness within society. Yet during this endeavour, one inevitably encounters minor deviations from expectations. For instance, where an examination result that should have been an A+ now yields merely an A. In such circumstances, the solution typically lies in persistently adjusting one's approach and modifying study methods.

However, in certain aspects of daily life, such as having one's bedding changed without permission, or inducing cognitive dissonance through the deliberate use of jargon to create information asymmetry, or employing persistent, repetitive tactics to overwhelm the victim with cognitive entropy until they concede verbally, or by co-opting third parties to seize legitimate resources from the other party, or through actions that leave one party feeling baffled—such as discovering a device hasn't fully charged after plugging it in—or by conveying signals through facial expressions that cause cognitive overload, preventing one party from sustaining focus on studies or tasks that could significantly enhance their capabilities.

This research paper uses the occurrence of damaged goods to illustrate the idealised approach to resolving such incidents, alongside the practical challenges encountered when implementing this approach in real-world scenarios.

## The most ideal scenario

When such situations arise, the ideal outcome would be for these issues to be resolved. However, in real life, based on my analysis of social dynamics, human perspectives are subject to change. Nevertheless, my research into social dynamics reveals that such incidents share common patterns. Among those frequently encountering these difficulties, several recurring traits emerge: a tendency towards social isolation, an inability to navigate challenges effectively, struggles with group integration, difficulties in communicating smoothly with peers, and a persistent lack of meaningful support from family when needed.

This situation may be manageable in the short term, but if left unresolved long-term, it could bring about the following changes to human cognition:

### Cognitive Load and Resource Depletion

When learning academic subjects, humans require an extraordinary degree of concentration and sustained repetition to achieve conditioned reflexes, that is, to form memories within the brain.

When such events recur with high frequency in an individual's life, their cognition gradually undergoes reshaping, fostering a sense of insecurity towards their surroundings. Cognitive therapy and psychological counselling prove remarkably limited in addressing this. Criminal psychology suggests that the only means to intervene in such behaviour is by making the consequences so severe that humans find them unbearable, thereby compelling them to abandon the action.

Those who endure such experiences subconsciously perceive society as unsafe, thereby developing complex post-traumatic stress disorder.

### Chronic Stress and Hippocampal Function

When an individual finds themselves in an environment where the future remains unpredictable for an extended period, the survival instinct often compels them to assimilate into the social milieu as a protective measure. This social environment may be benign, or it may harbour a small faction that deliberately fosters such chaos.

Moreover, prolonged exposure to such conditions leads to persistently elevated cortisol levels. Under these circumstances, the brain prioritises survival instincts (hypervigilance), thereby suppressing activity in regions responsible for higher-order logical analysis and deep learning.

### Learned helplessness and the failure of administrative feedback mechanisms

When rules within a social environment cannot be effectively enforced—for instance, where regulations prohibit drinking water during lessons, yet instances of students drinking in class go unchecked—people tend to conclude that such rules need not be observed.

Similarly, within a school setting, when an individual persistently experiences external pressures that cause personal discomfort, yet the third party responsible for oversight fails to intervene effectively due to a critical gap in the chain of evidence, this leads to cognitive dissonance for the affected party.

To summarise in a single sentence: when support mechanisms prove ineffective or even invite blame, individuals logically conclude that the environment is uncontrollable, leading to a breakdown in their academic engagement with that system.

## Current issues encountered

Having addressed the idealistic level, let us now turn to the practical difficulties encountered in real life.

In reality, when systems tackle such issues, they often require a closed-loop chain of evidence to delineate responsibility. While this closed-loop evidence chain appears highly reasonable and robust on the surface, its application in actual life frequently proves inadequate for resolving problems that arise immediately.

Take the loss of property, for instance. Under normal circumstances, this should be the easiest to resolve, as the chain of evidence hinges on the financial aspect – compensation for the loss suffices. Yet human behaviour is notoriously variable, defying a uniform standard for resolution. In judicial practice, mediation often serves merely as a buffer, failing to provide an effective solution.

As previously stated, human behaviour is inherently variable. Consider the example of parents urging their children to study diligently. The parents' intention is for their offspring to achieve independence through hard work. The children, however, are often preoccupied solely with their immediate feelings. Even when parents speak firmly with good intentions, children may reject the message out of fear.

Certain behaviours in children, such as a persistent refusal to heed parental advice, can prove deeply frustrating for parents. Over time, parental perspectives can become entrenched. The mere elevation of a parent's voice can trigger an unconscious assumption that the child is about to misbehave.
