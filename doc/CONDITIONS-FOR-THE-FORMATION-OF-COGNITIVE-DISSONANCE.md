## Conditions for the Formation of Cognitive Dissonance

> Here, we distinguish between two parties: Party A, who experiences these events, and Party B, who actively initiates them. Party B may be an individual or a group.

For instance, B might request A's assistance with a task. A is unwilling but struggles to find a suitable reason to decline. A is already quite busy, yet B, the initiator, appears to disregard A's desire to focus on their own affairs. Consequently, through repeated verbal reminders, B compels A to assist, effectively achieving a truce.

Occasional instances carry little significance, but frequent occurrences induce cognitive dissonance in A. The reasons are as follows:

- Power Compensation and Projection of Omnipotence

Within the confined social sphere of a dormitory, certain individuals—lacking control over external circumstances such as academic struggles or frequent life setbacks—develop increasingly childlike perspectives. They compensate by exerting control over others to project an illusion of omnipotence.

- Boundary Pressure Testing in Soft Bullying

This constitutes a probing algorithm where B tests A's tolerance thresholds to deeply model their personality traits, aiming to gauge the victim's boundary endurance. Should A exhibit insensitivity to the incident, the requests will escalate incrementally. This represents a form of incremental violation, though exceptions exist for genuinely unintentional cases.

- Forced Diversion of Cognitive Labour

When engaging in prolonged, focused knowledge acquisition, one must sustain concentrated thought on the learning process. When B commands A, B not only conserves their own physical energy but also forcibly occupies A's cognitive bandwidth. A must interrupt their current learning process to switch to executing B's rudimentary directives. This ‘interrupt handling’ incurs substantial performance degradation.

There exists another peculiar scenario: when B entices A to consume nicotine, it inflicts irreversible damage upon A's brain. As tobacco is frequently categorised as a Group 1 carcinogen, such substances typically possess no safe dosage.

At times, B employs a more insidious tactic: consciously neglecting A to instil passive aggression. This induces cognitive dissonance, compelling A to expend greater attention on B, thereby draining A's energy reserves. The home environment, however, serves as A's optimal sanctuary and the most conducive setting for learning and knowledge absorption.

## A Supplementary Note on Compliance Testing

The home inherently symbolises security; in contrast, school dormitories are communal spaces, while the domestic sphere is typically safeguarded by public authority.

For B's actions to induce cognitive dissonance in A, the following conditions must be met simultaneously or partially:  
1. When A initiates communication with B, B displays palpable impatience or repeated attempts at dialogue prove futile.  
2. When B communicates with A, they frequently rehash past issues or employ threatening tones to coerce A into compliance.  
3. When issuing commands, B frequently employs information asymmetry to coerce compliance from A.  
4. A exhibits pronounced avoidance tendencies when interacting with B.  
5. B possesses a poor social reputation among peers.  
6. A has developed mild psychological distress.  
7. A faces difficulties in gathering evidence and experiences a sense of being unable to articulate their grievances.  
8. During multiple exchanges, B demonstrates clear signs of hostility.  
9. B exhibited initial unfriendly behaviour towards A upon their first encounter.  
10. B displays amiable attitudes towards peers but targets A specifically, or encourages others to avoid interacting with A.  

From the above ten conditions, it is evident that the root causes of B's behaviour largely stem from their early family environment. These behavioural patterns suggest B predominantly experienced neglect during their upbringing. Parents consistently suppressed B's expression of negative emotions, leading to internalisation of this suppression. Consequently, B seeks familiarised validation by provoking external criticism.

The characteristics underlying A's submissive behaviour follow a similar logic, though the root causes of A's introversion are precisely the opposite of B's. A's personality readily suggests a family environment that prioritised academic achievement while neglecting interpersonal skills. Their parents themselves lacked the capacity to navigate relational conflicts. Consequently, the child—A—adopted this pattern. Moreover, individuals with A's personality type often exhibit exceptional academic prowess. This very excellence, however, tends to provoke jealousy in B.
