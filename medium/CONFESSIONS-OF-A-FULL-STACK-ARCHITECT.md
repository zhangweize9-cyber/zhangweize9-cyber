# Confessions of a Full-Stack Architect

> This article is a bit lengthy, and most of it is based on my personal experiences. To save readers time, I'll use many subheadings to summarize the content of each section.

## Introducing Myself
It's a pleasure to meet you all on this platform! I'm the-essence-of-life.  Currently 17 years old, I am pursuing a degree in Music Education at a teacher training college. This year marks my sophomore year, and I am continuing to delve deeper into the syntax knowledge related to programming.

## Back when I first started doing self-media
I used to make tutorials on Bilibili, probably for three or four years. At first, my tutorials were about setting up home theaters using Kodi. Back then, the only device I could tinker with at home was the TV, and what you could do on it was pretty limited. 

## All the devices I've played with before
Back then, I also had an MP3 player—an OPPO S9K. I'd often plug in headphones, use the TV to copy music and e-books onto it, and sometimes even use the TV itself. The TV's specs weren't great—basically a 1GB RAM + 8GB storage combo—but it could run Android 9, which was pretty decent for the time. 

## The process of creating content for self-media
I thought about recording tutorials on the TV, then using ADB to transfer them to my phone for editing. Back then, I felt like I had achieved something small, accumulating over 800 followers. I did content creation for about four years, but I never achieved the same level of success as other bloggers.

## Begin researching terminals
But I did stick with it for quite a while. During that time, I gradually weaned myself off the dependency on tutoring classes and shifted toward self-study, gaining enough confidence to learn programming on my own. When I first encountered the Linux terminal, I thought, “Wow, this is so hard!” My technical skills were pretty weak back then, so I just played around with those five commands over and over. Later, just to show off, I started using all kinds of plugins to make my terminal look flashy. At the time, I was developing purely on my phone because my home laptop was too underpowered.

## When starting to set up the development environment on an Android phone
I consistently used Termux:Monet for mobile development, installing Neovim and LSP on my phone. Even my SparenVim distribution was developed on that device. When I later switched hardware, I forgot to back up my public key, rendering that account inaccessible. With no other option, I had to create a new one.

## Take the Computer Level 1 Exam
Anyway, my approach to software development back then was always about optimizing software for the most extreme hardware. I hadn't taken the Level 1 Computer Exam at the time. Later, when I looked into what it covered—basically Word, Excel, PowerPoint, the office suite plus file operations—I decided to take it. The results weren't exactly stellar. I fell one point short of a “Good” grade. There are four levels of computer exams in our area, with Level 1 being the most basic computer exam.

## I plan to take the National Computer Rank Examination Level 2.
After that, my family wanted to enroll me in intensive training for the Level 2 exam, but it cost 2000 yuan. After discussing it with my family, I decided to go to the computer mall myself, pick out a laptop, and study on my own. My family agreed. I ended up choosing one with decent specs—an i7-1260p chip. Aside from the screen clarity, it performed quite well overall. I really have to thank my dad. He spent over 2000 yuan on that laptop to support my computer studies, and I was genuinely, truly touched.

## The first thing I did after getting the laptop
The first thing I did after getting my laptop was install the WSL2 virtual machine to start coding, since Linux makes dependency management much easier. The 16GB RAM + 512GB storage combo was perfectly sufficient for my needs. Plus, my English wasn't great back then, so I often needed translations to grasp the gist of official documentation. While others were learning sight-singing and ear training, I was diving into computer programming, figuring out how to create content for social media, and learning video editing. When it comes to music theory, I was practically clueless. But I've always been genuinely interested in computers.

## The Linux distribution I chose at the time
I remember when I first installed WSL2, I started with Ubuntu—after all, it's the default Linux system on my phone. Later, I noticed Ubuntu seemed to have slightly fewer software packages available on Windows laptops, so I switched to Arch Linux. I really appreciate how Arch Linux handles rolling updates for its packages. After that, I rewrote my entire Neovim configuration.

## The period when I was researching Neovim configurations
Initially, I wrote everything in pure Lua, merely calling the interfaces documented in Neovim's official documentation. Initially, I used vim-plug as my package manager, but later migrated to packer. I even posted about successfully achieving dual package manager coexistence. Eventually, I switched to lazy.nvim. At first, I barely understood this plugin manager's loading logic, but after consulting others online, I finally grasped it.

## The time when I started writing the Neovim distribution
Later on, I had pretty much mastered the configuration, so I thought about creating my own Neovim distribution. Unfortunately, it didn't gain much traction. Back then, I didn't know how to write official documentation. I was purely focused on coding. Later, after browsing more source code online, I realized projects really need comments. I wasn't keen on writing them at the time—my English wasn't great, and I genuinely didn't know how to approach it. Even variable names were just thrown together based on my limited English skills. It was all a bit of a mess.

## I'm starting to update the Neovim tutorial.
For a while, I uploaded nearly ten hours of tutorials on Bilibili about configuring Neovim from scratch. The longest episode was three hours long. Believe me, it wasn't just the viewers—sometimes even I would spend ages exporting three-hour videos using Adobe Premiere Pro, and uploading them would take another 6-8 hours. The most exhausting part back then was manually editing the embedded subtitles. I spent over an hour on it before finally giving up and just exporting the video as-is.

## For a while, I started researching how to optimize Neovim's performance using FFI interfaces.
Later on, when I ran out of fresh content to update, I decided to post some prank videos on Bilibili. The approach I took was pretty niche—optimizing Neovim's startup speed using FFI. My idea was to offload time-consuming tasks to asynchronous loading via FFI. After that video went live, I noticed a significant spike in visitors.

## The time when I started contributing to open source on GitHub
Recently, I started a new GitHub account to try maintaining some open-source projects. Since the programming language I'm self-learning, C++, has a distinct characteristic that I personally find slightly challenging, I thought I'd take a shortcut by contributing to open-source projects like the LLVM project, Neovim, and Microsoft Terminal by opening pull requests. At first, I struggled to understand the code. Only after consistently contributing for a while—which is where I am now—have I started to grasp a tiny bit of the syntax. As you could probably tell from my previous article on C++ syntax, I'll be honest... I'm still not very good at C++.

## This final section is a brief reflection on my past struggles.
Lately, I've come to realize a truth: learning is like rowing upstream—if you don't advance, you fall behind. Though I've invested immense effort already, I'm still far from being battle-ready. So the path ahead is one I must walk on my own.
